Beyond Clean Theorem Text: Evaluating Theorem Unit
Construction from Noisy OCR JSON for Mathematical
Auto-Formalization
Anonymous Authors
NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations & Datasets Track Preview
Abstract. Mathematical auto-formalization is typically evaluated under a clean-input assumption:
theorem statements are treated as already segmented, readable, and symbolically stable before formal
translation begins. However, realistic document workflows rarely satisfy this assumption. Mathematical
textbooks and papers are often first processed by OCR and layout parsers, yielding noisy JSON structures
with fragmented formulas, broken reading order, corrupted symbols, and ambiguous theorem boundaries.
As a result, failures attributed to downstream formalizers may in fact originate from upstream input
construction.
We introduce theorem unit construction from noisy OCR JSON as an evaluation problem
for mathematical auto-formalization. Rather than assuming access to clean theorem text, we study
whether a system can recover formalization-ready theorem units—structured objects that preserve
statement text, formula ownership, unit type, and source provenance—from OCR-derived document
representations. To support this setting, we define a theorem-unit schema, construct a benchmark from
mathematical textbook pages, and propose an evaluation protocol spanning three dimensions: structural
recovery, statement integrity, and downstream formalization readiness.
Using a probability-theory textbook as a case study, we compare rule-based, LLM-based, and hybrid
constructors, and analyze how input construction quality affects a fixed downstream theorem-processing
stage. Across metrics for boundary recovery, formula attachment, statement completeness, and down-
stream readiness, we find that explicit structure-aware theorem unit construction substantially improves
the quality and usability of theorem inputs. Our results suggest that clean theorem text should not be
treated as a default evaluation premise in mathematical auto-formalization. Instead, OCR-to-theorem
reconstruction should be recognized as a first-class benchmark layer for evaluating document-grounded
formal reasoning systems.

1 Introduction
Mathematical auto-formalization is commonly evalu-
ated under a clean-input assumption: theorem state-
ments are treated as already segmented, readable,
and symbolically stable before formal translation
begins. This assumption has been productive for
studying theorem translation itself, but it removes a
realistic and consequential source of difficulty from
evaluation. In many document workflows, mathemat-
ical content is not directly available as clean theorem
text. Instead, it is first extracted from PDFs by OCR
and layout parsers, yielding noisy JSON structures
with fragmented formulas, unstable reading order,
corrupted symbols, ambiguous theorem boundaries,
and uncertain attachments between prose and dis-
played mathematics. When theorem inputs are dam-
aged at this stage, downstream failures may reflect
errors in input construction as much as deficiencies
in the formalizer.
This paper studies that missing benchmark layer.
Rather than assuming access to clean theorem
text, we ask whether a system can reconstruct
formalization-ready theorem units from OCR-derived
document representations. A theorem unit is a struc-
tured theorem-like object intended for downstream
mathematical processing: it preserves statement text,
formula ownership, unit type, and source provenance.
Our focus is therefore not full theorem proving and
not end-to-end Lean generation. Instead, we evaluate
the upstream step that determines whether theorem-
level inputs are coherent enough to be consumed by
later formalization stages.
This setting matters because mathematical mean-
ing is unusually sensitive to document structure. A

by OCR fragmentation; a displayed formula may be
attached to the wrong local span; a notation-bearing
sentence may be omitted; or proof text may be ab-
sorbed into the theorem statement. Such errors are
not merely cosmetic. In mathematics, small struc-
tural or symbolic distortions can alter scope, notation,
quantification, or even the statement being formal-
ized. Yet current evaluations of auto-formalization
rarely test robustness to these document-grounded
failures. As a result, they risk overstating the qual-
ity of downstream formalization inputs by beginning
from already cleaned statements.
We therefore introduce theorem unit construc-
tion from noisy OCR JSON as an evaluation
problem for mathematical auto-formalization. Given
OCR- and layout-derived page representations, the
task is to recover theorem-like units such as defini-
tions, theorems, lemmas, propositions, corollaries,
and mathematically substantive examples, together
with their normalized statement content, attached
formulas, and source-block provenance. The goal
is not generic document extraction, but reconstruc-
tion of theorem-level objects suitable for downstream
formal reasoning pipelines.
To support this setting, we make three benchmark-
oriented contributions. First, we define a structured
theorem-unit schema that makes explicit which fields
are required for downstream theorem processing. Sec-
ond, we construct a benchmark from mathematical
textbook pages in which the inputs remain OCR-
derived and noisy, while the targets are formalization-
ready theorem units. Third, we introduce an eval-
uation protocol that measures theorem-unit quality
along three complementary dimensions: structural
recovery, statement integrity, and downstream for-
malization readiness. This layered evaluation is im-
portant because a system can appear adequate under
surface extraction metrics while still producing brittle
or unusable theorem inputs.
As an enabling system for this benchmark, we
implement a structure-aware theorem unit construc-
tor that performs layout-aware normalization, math-
text alignment, theorem boundary detection, OCR-
sensitive correction, and theorem unit assembly. We
compare this system with rule-based, LLM-only, and
hybrid baselines on a probability-theory case study.
More importantly, we use these systems to analyze
a broader scientific question: whether differences in
upstream input construction lead to materially differ-
ent downstream conclusions under a fixed theorem-
processing backend.
Our results suggest that they do. Systems that
better preserve document structure produce theorem
units with higher boundary quality, more reliable
formula attachment, greater statement completeness,
and substantially better downstream readiness than
raw OCR text or weaker theorem extraction base-
lines. These findings imply that some difficulty com-
monly attributed to theorem formalizers may origi-
nate earlier, at the point where theorem inputs are
reconstructed from noisy document structure. More
broadly, they suggest that clean theorem text should
not be treated as a default evaluation premise for
document-grounded mathematical formalization.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We identify a benchmark gap in mathematical
auto-formalization: current evaluations often as-
sume clean theorem text and thereby omit a real-
istic upstream source of failure.
• We introduce theorem unit construction from noisy
OCR JSON as a new evaluation task for document-
grounded mathematical formalization.
• We define a theorem-unit schema, build a bench-
mark from OCR-derived mathematical pages, and
propose a layered evaluation protocol covering
structural recovery, statement integrity, and down-
stream readiness.
• We show that upstream input construction materi-
ally affects downstream theorem usability under a
fixed theorem-processing interface, reframing part
of the apparent difficulty of auto-formalization as
an input-construction problem.
2 Related Work
2.1 Mathematical auto-formalization under
clean-input assumptions
Recent progress in mathematical auto-formalization
has been driven by large language models, aligned the-
orem datasets, and increasingly structured generation
pipelines. Prior work has shown that non-trivial por-
tions of informal mathematics can be translated into
formal statements, and benchmark efforts have made
this problem more systematic by providing aligned
pairs of natural-language theorems and formal tar-
gets [7, 1]. More recent systems further improve this
setting through retrieval, decomposition, compiler-
guided reflection, and theorem-oriented interaction
loops [3, 6].
Despite these advances, most existing evaluations
share an important assumption: the theorem state-
ment is already available in a reasonably clean and
segmented form.
In other words, the benchmark
typically begins after the theorem has already been
extracted from the document and converted into
a stable textual object. This assumption is useful

for isolating the translation problem itself, but it
also removes a realistic upstream source of failure
from evaluation. Our work is motivated by this gap.
Rather than studying theorem translation from al-
ready cleaned inputs, we study the prior stage at
which theorem-level inputs must be reconstructed
from OCR-derived document structure.
2.2 Structure, retrieval, and theorem-oriented
processing pipelines
A growing line of work emphasizes that successful
mathematical reasoning systems depend not only on
generation quality, but also on retrieval, structure,
decomposition, and consistency checking. These sys-
tems highlight the importance of intermediate repre-
sentations and structured processing in formalization
pipelines. They are closely related to our work in
spirit because they reject the view that theorem for-
malization is a purely one-shot generation problem.
However, the layer at which we intervene is differ-
ent. Existing structure-aware formalization pipelines
generally operate after a theorem-like input has al-
ready been identified. By contrast, we study how
such an input should be reconstructed in the first
place when the available evidence is a noisy OCR-
and layout-derived page representation. Our contri-
bution is therefore not a replacement for theorem
translation systems, but an evaluation layer that
precedes them.
2.3 OCR, layout analysis, and document un-
derstanding
Outside the formalization literature, document intel-
ligence has made substantial progress in OCR, layout
analysis, formula recognition, and structured recov-
ery of scientific documents [5, 4, 2]. These works are
highly relevant because they address page segmenta-
tion, reading order, multimodal page structure, and
document-level parsing under visual noise. Such ad-
vances provide important tools and representations
for working with mathematical documents in the
wild.
At the same time, generic document understanding
objectives do not fully capture the needs of theorem-
oriented downstream use. A page can be adequately
parsed for reading or indexing while still failing to
yield a usable theorem input object for later for-
malization. In particular, theorem-unit construction
requires decisions about boundary resolution, for-
mula ownership, proof exclusion, and provenance
preservation that are not typically central to generic
document parsing benchmarks. Our work therefore
sits between document understanding and theorem
formalization: it adapts document-derived inputs to
the specific requirements of theorem-level mathemat-
ical reasoning.
2.4 Evaluation gaps in document-grounded
mathematical reasoning
Our work is most directly related to recent efforts
that question whether existing benchmarks faithfully
capture the real operational conditions of reason-
ing systems. In many domains, strong performance
on clean or simplified inputs can overstate robust-
ness when systems are deployed on noisier, partially
structured, or tool-generated representations. Math-
ematical auto-formalization appears to face the same
issue. If evaluation begins only from clean theorem
text, then a significant portion of practical difficulty—
namely, the reconstruction of theorem inputs from
documents—is excluded by design.
We view theorem unit construction from noisy
OCR JSON as a response to this evaluation gap.
The goal is not to compete with downstream formal-
ization benchmarks, but to complement them with an
upstream layer that exposes a realistic failure mode.
In this sense, our benchmark is intended to broaden
what counts as evaluative evidence for document-
grounded mathematical reasoning systems.
2.5 Our positioning
This paper sits at the intersection of document pars-
ing and mathematical auto-formalization, but its
primary contribution is evaluative rather than purely
algorithmic.
We contribute a task definition, a
theorem-unit schema, a benchmark built from OCR-
derived mathematical pages, and a layered evaluation
protocol spanning structural recovery, statement in-
tegrity, and downstream readiness. The accompany-
ing structure-aware constructor serves as an enabling
system that makes these evaluations concrete.
To our knowledge, prior work has not centered
the following combination: evaluating theorem recon-
struction directly from noisy OCR JSON, represent-
ing theorem-like outputs as formalization-oriented
structured units, and measuring how different input
constructions change downstream theorem usability
under a fixed backend. Our paper is therefore best
understood as introducing a missing benchmark layer
for document-grounded mathematical formalization
rather than as proposing a standalone replacement
for theorem translation systems.
3 Problem Formulation
Mathematical auto-formalization is often framed as
a mapping from informal theorem statements to for-
mal languages such as Lean. In realistic document
pipelines, however, the input available to a formal-
ization system is frequently not a clean theorem
statement but the output of OCR and layout pars-
3

ing applied to mathematical PDFs. These outputs
are typically represented as noisy JSON structures
containing text blocks, formula blocks, coordinates,
confidence values, and partial reading-order infor-
mation. Our goal is to study the gap between such
document-derived representations and the theorem-
level inputs expected by downstream formalization
systems.
3.1 Input representation
We assume that each source page has been processed
by an OCR engine and a layout parser. The resulting
page is represented as a JSON object containing a
sequence of OCR blocks. Each block may correspond
to running text, a theorem heading, a displayed for-
mula, a section title, an example, a proof fragment,
or another layout element. In addition to textual
content, blocks may include identifiers, coordinates,
ordering metadata, parser-generated type hints, and
OCR confidence scores.
Crucially, we do not assume that these parser-
generated structures are correct.
The input may
contain broken reading order, block fragmentation,
over-merging, symbol corruption, or incorrect sepa-
ration between theorem statements and neighboring
exposition. This noisy OCR JSON is the benchmark
input. We do not replace it with manually cleaned
theorem text.
3.2 Theorem unit construction as an evalua-
tion task
We define theorem unit construction from noisy
OCR JSON as follows. Given a page-level OCR
JSON representation, the system must produce a
set of structured theorem-like units suitable for
downstream theorem processing. Each output unit
should correspond to a mathematically meaningful
statement-like object that could plausibly serve as
an input to later formalization stages.
The task differs from generic document segmenta-
tion in two ways. First, the target object is theorem-
oriented rather than page-oriented: the system must
reconstruct theorem-like units rather than simply
label blocks. Second, success is not determined only
by textual readability. The output must preserve the
structural and symbolic information necessary for
downstream theorem consumption, including state-
ment content, formula ownership, unit type, and
source provenance.
We evaluate theorem unit construction not merely
as an extraction problem but as an evaluation
layer for mathematical auto-formalization.
The
scientific question is whether systems can recover
formalization-ready inputs from realistic document
representations, and whether failures at this stage
materially affect downstream conclusions.
3.3 Theorem unit schema
A theorem unit is a structured object designed to
represent a theorem-like mathematical statement in
a form usable by downstream systems. Each theorem
unit contains at least the following fields:
• unit_type: the semantic type of the unit (e.g.,
definition, theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary,
example);
• title: the local title or numbered heading when
present;
• statement_text: the normalized theorem state-
ment text;
• formula_spans: the displayed or inline formulas
attached to the statement;
• source_blocks: the OCR blocks from which the
unit was reconstructed;
• reading_order: the block order used in assem-
bly;
• optional metadata: such as confidence score or
parser flags.
This schema is intentionally formalization-oriented.
It is not meant to be a generic cleaned text span, but
a theorem-level input object that preserves both con-
tent and provenance. The inclusion of provenance
is particularly important for error analysis, down-
stream auditing, and future systems that may wish
to re-ground theorem units in their source pages.
3.4 Task challenges
The task is challenging for several reasons.
First, mathematical meaning is distributed across
multiple modalities, including prose, inline notation,
displayed equations, numbering, and layout cues.
Recovering a theorem unit therefore requires more
than linear text cleanup.
Second, theorem boundaries are often ambiguous
in OCR output. A theorem heading may be detached
from its body, a proof marker may be weakened or
omitted, and a displayed formula may interrupt the
local flow of text. Boundary errors can therefore arise
even when most local content is correctly recognized.
Third, OCR errors in mathematical documents
are highly non-uniform. Symbol corruption tends
to concentrate on quantifiers, indices, delimiters,
Greek letters, operators, and other notation-sensitive
spans. Generic OCR correction can improve read-
ability while still leaving theorem inputs unusable
downstream.

dimensional. An output can achieve reasonable sur-

3.5 Relation to downstream formalization
This paper focuses on theorem unit construction
as an upstream infrastructure problem. We do not
attempt full theorem proving, chapter-aware context
recovery, or end-to-end Lean generation. Instead, we
isolate the stage at which theorem-level inputs are
reconstructed from noisy document structure and
study it as a standalone bottleneck for mathematical
formalization.
This choice should be interpreted carefully. Our
benchmark does not replace full formalization eval-
uation. Rather, it measures the quality of theorem
inputs under OCR-derived document noise.
The
intended claim is therefore not that theorem unit
construction solves mathematical auto-formalization,
but that it constitutes a missing and consequential
benchmark layer for evaluating document-grounded
formal reasoning systems.
4 Benchmark Construction and Evalu-
ation Protocol
To evaluate theorem unit construction under realistic
document noise, we build a benchmark from OCR-
derived mathematical pages rather than from already
cleaned theorem statements. This design is deliber-
ate. If the goal is to understand how mathemati-
cal formalization behaves under document-grounded
noise, then the benchmark must preserve the up-
stream errors introduced by OCR and layout parsing
instead of removing them in advance.
As a case study, we focus on a probability-theory
textbook containing theorem-like material across
foundational topics such as measure theory, inde-
pendence, integration, convergence, martingales, and
stochastic processes. The source is well suited to
this benchmark because it exhibits the full range
of document phenomena relevant to theorem recon-
struction: numbered theorem environments, formula-
dense statements, notation-bearing definitions, exam-
ples intertwined with exposition, and proof-adjacent
text.
4.1 Benchmark construction principle
The central benchmark principle is to preserve a re-
alistic mismatch between input and target. Inputs
are noisy OCR- and layout-derived page-level JSON.
Targets are formalization-ready theorem units an-
notated at the theorem level. Human annotation
is used to define gold theorem units and structural
supervision, but not to clean the inputs into already
usable theorem text. In this way, the benchmark
exposes the actual reconstruction gap that down-
stream formalization systems would face in realistic
document workflows.
4.2 Source documents and scope
We construct the current benchmark from pages sam-
pled from the early chapters of the textbook, with
emphasis on chapters containing diverse theorem-like
material and frequent interactions between text and
displayed mathematics. The present version focuses
on Chapters 1, 2, 4, and 5, corresponding to pages
13–124 of the source textbook. These pages contain
definitions, theorems, lemmas, propositions, corollar-
ies, examples, explanatory prose, and proof-adjacent
material, making them suitable for studying OCR-
to-theorem reconstruction under realistic structural
noise.

4.4 Annotation protocol
To ensure that the benchmark is useful for evaluation
rather than only for descriptive page annotation, we
adopt a theorem-oriented annotation protocol.
Unit identification.
Annotators inspect the
OCR JSON together with the rendered page image
and identify candidate theorem-like regions.
Boundary resolution. For each candidate unit,
annotators determine the start and end boundaries in
terms of source OCR blocks, paying special attention
to detached headings, inserted formulas, and theorem–
proof transitions.
Unit typing. Each unit is assigned a semantic
type label.
When local formatting and semantic
role disagree, annotation prioritizes the document’s
labeling convention when present and otherwise falls
back to the mathematical role of the span.
Formula attachment. Displayed and inline for-
mulas are attached to a theorem unit if they belong
to the statement rather than to proof text or sur-
rounding exposition.
Statement normalization. Annotators produce
a normalized statement representation that corrects
OCR artifacts which do not change the mathematical
content, while preserving the wording and notation
needed for theorem reconstruction.
Provenance recording. For each unit, annota-
tion records source blocks and local order to enable
auditing and downstream alignment.
Because theorem reconstruction can be ambiguous,
we also include a pilot round, guideline refinement,
and double annotation on a subset of pages. This is
important not only for data quality but also for clar-
ifying what the benchmark is intended to measure.
4.5 Data splits
We partition the benchmark into train, development,
and test splits at the page level. Page-level splitting
prevents systems from seeing near-duplicate local
contexts across training and evaluation data, which
would otherwise inflate performance. Where possible,
we also preserve local section coherence to reduce
leakage from adjacent textbook pages.
The current split contains 48 training pages, 12 de-
velopment pages, and 15 test pages, with 312, 79, and
96 theorem units respectively. In total, the bench-
mark contains 75 pages, 5043 OCR blocks, and 487
theorem units, with 62.9% of units spanning multi-
ple OCR blocks. These statistics confirm that the
task cannot be reduced to local heading extraction
or single-block classification.
4.6 Evaluation dimensions
The benchmark is designed to support evaluation
along three complementary dimensions.
Structural recovery. This dimension measures
whether the correct theorem-like object is recon-
structed from OCR JSON. Representative metrics
include boundary precision, boundary recall, bound-
ary F1, unit-type accuracy, and block assignment
accuracy.
Statement integrity. This dimension measures
whether the internal content of the unit remains
mathematically usable. Representative metrics in-

Downstream formalization readiness. This

4.7 Benchmark tasks
We define three benchmark tasks corresponding to
different levels of evaluation: Task A: Boundary
Detection, Task B: Theorem Unit Assembly,
and Task C: Downstream Readiness Evalua-
tion. In this paper, Task B is the main end-to-end
benchmark objective, while Tasks A and C serve
diagnostic and utility-oriented roles.
5 A Structure-Aware Theorem Unit
Constructor
To instantiate this benchmark setting, we imple-
ment a structure-aware theorem unit constructor that
serves as an enabling system for evaluating OCR-to-
theorem reconstruction. The role of this system is
twofold: first, it provides a practical method for pro-
ducing theorem units from noisy OCR JSON; second,
it enables controlled comparisons of how different
input-construction strategies affect downstream the-
orem usability.
5.1 Design principle
The central design principle is to preserve document
structure rather than flatten it away. Mathemati-
cal theorem statements are often distributed across
headings, prose, inline notation, displayed equations,
and local layout cues. Flattening OCR output into
plain text at the outset discards information that
is critical for theorem reconstruction, especially in
the presence of detached formulas, ambiguous bound-
aries, and OCR-sensitive symbol corruption. Our
method therefore maintains block-level and formula-
level structure throughout the pipeline and uses these
signals to build theorem-oriented output objects.
5.2 Overview
The constructor consists of five stages: (1) layout-
aware JSON normalization, (2) math-text alignment,
(3) theorem boundary detection, (4) OCR-sensitive
correction, and (5) theorem unit assembly. These
stages are designed to map a noisy page-level OCR
JSON representation into a structured theorem-unit
object while preserving provenance and minimizing
unsupported rewriting.
5.3 Layout-aware JSON normalization
The raw OCR JSON often contains noisy block order-
ings, inconsistent block labels, duplicated whitespace,
and fragmented text or math spans. The normal-
ization stage converts this raw input into a stable
intermediate representation suitable for theorem re-
construction.
In our implementation, reading-order repair is
based on geometric ordering cues, heading heuristics,
and local continuity. Conservative block merging is
applied only when adjacent blocks exhibit compatible
alignment, semantic continuation, and no strong evi-
dence of theorem-boundary separation. The purpose
of normalization is not to fully recover the original
document, but to reduce spurious structural noise
while preserving evidence needed for later decisions.
5.4 Math-text alignment
A theorem statement in mathematical writing is
frequently distributed across prose and displayed
formulas. We therefore perform explicit alignment
between text blocks and formula blocks before the-
orem assembly. Candidate attachments are scored
using geometric compatibility, lexical continuity, lo-
cal punctuation, numbering cues, and candidate-span
context.
This stage is particularly important for detached
displayed equations, which are often visually centered
and structurally ambiguous in OCR outputs. With-
out explicit alignment, systems tend either to omit
formulas entirely or to attach them to neighboring
proof or exposition spans.
5.5 Theorem boundary detection
We formulate boundary detection as span prediction
over normalized OCR blocks. Candidate spans are
proposed using structural signals such as theorem
headings, numbering patterns, indentation, unit-type
keywords, formula density, and local block continuity.
Candidate spans are then validated and reranked to
discourage over-merging, proof leakage, and spurious
theorem fragmentation.
The purpose of this stage is not only to identify
theorem boundaries, but also to preserve theorem-
level coherence. In particular, boundary detection
must decide whether nearby formulas and adjacent
blocks belong to the same theorem statement, to a
proof, or to surrounding exposition.

5.6 OCR-sensitive correction
After candidate theorem spans are identified, we
apply minimal OCR-sensitive correction. The objec-
tive is to repair obvious symbol corruption without
paraphrasing or hallucinating missing content. Sus-
picious regions are identified using OCR confidence,
malformed formula patterns, unusual delimiter struc-
tures, and notation-sensitive heuristics. Corrections
are constrained to preserve mathematical meaning,
theorem structure, and source-block identity when-
ever possible.
This minimal-correction design is important for
evaluation. If unrestricted rewriting were allowed, a
system could improve surface fluency while obscur-
ing whether it truly reconstructed the theorem from
document evidence.
5.7 Theorem unit assembly
The final stage assembles the structured theorem-unit
object from normalized content, aligned formulas,
predicted type, corrected spans, and source prove-
nance. Confidence aggregation combines evidence
from boundary quality, formula alignment, correction
reliability, and unit-typing consistency to produce a
final theorem-unit output. The result is a theorem-
oriented object rather than plain cleaned text.
5.8 Baseline families
To study theorem reconstruction as an evaluation
problem rather than as a single-system showcase, we
compare four input-construction strategies: Rule-
based, LLM-only, Hybrid baseline, and the full
Structure-aware constructor. These baselines
represent qualitatively different assumptions about
how theorem inputs should be reconstructed, making
them suitable for studying how input construction
affects downstream evaluation.
6 Evaluative Experiments and Analysis
We evaluate theorem unit construction as a bench-
mark layer for mathematical auto-formalization, ask-
ing how different input-construction strategies affect
both theorem reconstruction quality and downstream
theorem usability.
6.1 Experimental questions
Our experiments address four questions:
• RQ1. Can theorem-like units be reliably recon-
structed from noisy OCR JSON at the page level?
• RQ2. Which sources of difficulty—boundary am-

6.2 Experimental setup
Data and scope. All experiments are conducted
on the benchmark described in Section 4, built from
OCR-derived JSON pages of a probability-theory
textbook. We use the page-level train/dev/test split
and do not provide systems with manually cleaned
theorem text during evaluation.
Input settings. To test the role of theorem-input
construction, we compare four settings: raw OCR
text, weak theorem-unit construction, a stronger hy-
brid baseline, and our structure-aware constructor.
This comparison is central to the paper: by varying
only the form of the theorem input, we can study how
upstream reconstruction quality changes downstream
conclusions.
Downstream interface. For downstream anal-
ysis, all constructed theorem inputs are passed to
the same fixed Structured Theorem Parser.
The
downstream component does not perform full the-
orem proving; instead, it checks whether theorem
inputs are structurally coherent, symbolically stable,
formula-complete, and compatible with theorem-level
downstream consumption. Holding this component
fixed allows us to isolate the effect of input quality
from downstream model variation.
Implementation notes. Rule-based baselines
use deterministic structural heuristics. LLM-based
components use fixed prompt templates and JSON-
constrained outputs. Hyperparameters, thresholds,
and prompt templates are finalized on the develop-
ment split and held constant on the test split. The
full system uses layout-aware normalization, explicit
formula alignment, hybrid boundary detection, mini-
mal OCR-sensitive correction, and confidence-aware
theorem-unit assembly.
6.3 Evaluation metrics
We organize evaluation into three layers.
Structural recovery. We report boundary preci-
sion, boundary recall, boundary F1, unit-type accu-
racy, and block assignment accuracy. These metrics
measure whether the system reconstructs the correct
theorem-like object from OCR JSON.
Statement integrity. We report formula attach-
ment accuracy, statement completeness, and normal-
ized edit distance. These metrics measure whether
the internal content of the theorem unit remains

mathematically usable after reconstruction.
Downstream readiness. We report structured
parse success, symbol stability, retrieval compatibil-
ity, and a composite formalization-readiness score.
These metrics measure whether predicted theorem
units can serve as stable theorem-level inputs for
later formalization stages.
This layered metric design is important because
theorem reconstruction failures are not interchange-
able. Some primarily harm structural recovery, while
others leave surface extraction metrics relatively in-
tact but severely reduce downstream usability.
6.4 Main benchmark results
The main results show that theorem unit construc-
tion is not a superficial preprocessing step. Systems
that better preserve document structure achieve con-
sistent gains not only in boundary recovery and for-
mula attachment, but also in downstream theorem
usability. In particular, the gap between raw OCR-
based inputs and structure-aware theorem units is
especially large under downstream-readiness metrics,
indicating that a substantial portion of failure can
be introduced before formal translation begins.
Compared with rule-based and weak theorem ex-
traction baselines, stronger structure-aware recon-
struction yields more stable theorem boundaries, bet-
ter formula ownership decisions, and more complete
statement objects. The hybrid baseline narrows part
of this gap, suggesting that language models can
help resolve local ambiguity, but explicit structural
grounding remains necessary for reliable theorem-
unit assembly. These findings support our central
claim: evaluations that begin from already cleaned
theorem statements omit a meaningful and conse-
quential source of difficulty in document-grounded
mathematical formalization.
6.5 Factor analysis via ablation
We use ablations as a factor analysis of theorem-unit
evaluation. Rather than asking only which compo-
nent improves aggregate performance, we ask which
dimensions of theorem reconstruction matter most
for downstream usability.
Removing normalization and explicit formula align-
ment causes the largest drops in end-to-end quality,
showing that local text extraction alone is insufficient
for building theorem-level inputs from OCR JSON.
By contrast, removing OCR-sensitive correction has a
smaller effect on some structural metrics but a larger
effect on downstream readiness, indicating that sym-
bolic stability becomes especially important once the-
orem units are consumed by later theorem-processing
interfaces. Confidence-aware assembly provides an
additional benefit by filtering out locally plausible
but globally inconsistent theorem reconstructions.
These patterns show that theorem-unit evaluation
should not rely on any single extraction metric in
isolation. High-quality theorem inputs require jointly
preserving structure, internal statement integrity,
and downstream usability.
6.6 Downstream readiness and evaluation bias
A central question of this paper is whether up-
stream input construction changes downstream con-
clusions.
To test this, we hold the downstream
theorem-processing component fixed and vary only
the form of the input representation. The results
show a strong dependence on theorem-input quality:
structure-aware theorem units are substantially more
parseable, symbolically stable, and retrievable than
raw OCR text or weak theorem extraction outputs.
This finding has an important evaluative implica-
tion. When auto-formalization systems are assessed
only from clean theorem text, part of the practical
difficulty of document-grounded formalization is re-
moved from the benchmark. As a result, performance
measured under clean-input settings may overesti-
mate the quality of theorem inputs available in re-
alistic pipelines. Our results therefore suggest that
OCR-to-theorem reconstruction should be treated
as a standard benchmark layer for evaluating math-
ematical reasoning systems that operate over real
documents.
6.7 Error analysis
We manually analyze remaining failures and group
them into four recurring categories: boundary ambi-
guity, formula-ownership ambiguity, OCR-sensitive
symbol corruption, and context leakage. These cate-
gories are not equally visible under standard extrac-
tion metrics. For example, some boundary failures
are obvious at the structural level, whereas symbol
corruption and formula misattachment may preserve
local readability while still rendering a theorem unit
unusable downstream.
This error analysis reinforces the value of the
benchmark. It exposes which aspects of document-
grounded mathematical reasoning remain under-
tested by clean-input evaluations and helps distin-
guish failures of theorem reconstruction from failures
of downstream formalization itself.

7 Discussion and Limitations
Our results suggest that theorem unit construction is
not merely a preprocessing convenience, but a distinct
and consequential evaluation layer for mathematical
auto-formalization. Existing benchmarks often begin
from clean theorem text, which is reasonable when
the goal is to isolate translation from informal math-
ematics to a formal language. However, this design
choice also removes a realistic upstream source of
brittleness. In real document workflows, theorem
statements must often be reconstructed from OCR-
and layout-derived representations before any theo-
rem translation can begin. Our experiments show
that the quality of this reconstruction materially af-

This point has an important implication for how
results in mathematical reasoning should be inter-
preted. Some failures commonly attributed to down-
stream formalizers may in fact originate earlier, at the
stage where theorem-level inputs are assembled from
noisy page structure. Conversely, strong performance
under clean-input evaluations may overestimate ro-
bustness in settings where theorem statements are
not given directly, but must be recovered from real
documents. We do not argue that clean-theorem
benchmarks are misguided; they remain essential for
isolating theorem translation and proof-oriented rea-
soning. Rather, we argue that they are incomplete
when the intended application involves document-
grounded theorem acquisition.
A second implication concerns benchmark design.
Our study suggests that evaluation should distin-
guish at least three layers of performance: whether
the correct theorem-like object is identified, whether
its internal mathematical content is preserved, and
whether the result is usable by downstream theorem-
processing components.

but not interchangeable. A system may achieve rea-

ing theorem units that are incomplete, symbolically
unstable, or poorly grounded in the source docu-
ment. For this reason, we view structural recovery,
statement integrity, and downstream readiness as
complementary dimensions rather than competing
alternatives.
At the same time, our study has several limitations.
First, the current benchmark is built from a single
textbook domain, namely probability theory. While
this domain is rich in theorem-like structure and
formula-bearing statements, it does not exhaust the
diversity of mathematical writing. Other areas may
exhibit different notation regimes, denser symbolic ex-
pressions, weaker theorem formatting conventions, or
more complex dependencies across pages and chap-
ters. The present benchmark should therefore be
interpreted as a case study demonstrating the impor-
tance of the OCR-to-theorem reconstruction gap, not
as a final universal benchmark for all mathematical
documents.
Second, the current task formulation is intention-
ally local. We focus on theorem-unit recovery at
the page level rather than full context reconstruction
across chapters, notation scopes, or long-range depen-
dencies. This simplification is useful for isolating the
benchmark layer studied here, but it also leaves out
broader contextual information that may matter for
complete formalization. Future work should examine
how theorem-unit construction interacts with local
notation declarations, preceding definitions, proof
dependencies, and chapter-level structure.
Third, our downstream analysis is based on a fixed
Structured Theorem Parser rather than full theo-
rem proving or end-to-end Lean generation. This is
a deliberate design choice: our goal is to measure
whether theorem inputs are structurally coherent
and usable downstream, not to solve the entire auto-
formalization problem in one paper. Accordingly,
downstream readiness should be interpreted as a
utility-oriented proxy for theorem-input quality, not
as a substitute for full formal correctness. A natural
next step is to couple this benchmark layer more
tightly with actual formal translation systems and
proof assistants.
Fourth, OCR-sensitive correction in mathematical
documents remains fundamentally difficult. Some
symbol errors are locally ambiguous, and some losses
cannot be repaired without document context or
prior mathematical knowledge. Our current formula-
tion intentionally constrains correction to minimal,
structure-preserving edits in order to keep the eval-
uation focused on reconstruction rather than unre-
stricted rewriting. This makes the benchmark more
interpretable, but it also means that certain hard
cases remain unsolved.
Finally, our work does not claim that theorem unit
construction is the only missing benchmark layer in
mathematical reasoning. Rather, it identifies one im-
portant omitted layer between document parsing and
theorem translation. More comprehensive evaluation
may eventually require multi-page context recovery,
notation tracking, theorem dependency resolution,
and theorem-to-proof coupling. We view the current
benchmark as a first step toward a broader eval-
uation stack for document-grounded mathematical
reasoning.
Taken together, these limitations also clarify the in-
tended scope of our claims. We do not claim to solve
mathematical auto-formalization end to end. We
claim that evaluation of document-grounded auto-
formalization is incomplete if it begins only from
clean theorem text, and that OCR-to-theorem re-
construction should be treated as a measurable and
consequential part of the problem.
8 Conclusion
We introduced theorem unit construction from
noisy OCR JSON as a new evaluation problem
for mathematical auto-formalization. Rather than
assuming access to clean theorem text, we studied
the more realistic setting in which theorem inputs
must be reconstructed from OCR- and layout-derived
document representations containing fragmented for-
mulas, unstable reading order, corrupted symbols,
and ambiguous theorem boundaries.
To support this setting, we defined a formalization-
oriented theorem-unit schema, constructed a bench-
mark from mathematical textbook pages, and pro-
posed a layered evaluation protocol spanning struc-
tural recovery, statement integrity, and downstream
formalization readiness. We further instantiated this
benchmark with structure-aware, rule-based, LLM-
based, and hybrid theorem constructors in order to
analyze how different input-construction strategies
affect theorem-level downstream usability.
Our results show that the quality of theorem recon-
struction materially changes downstream conclusions.
Systems that preserve document structure more effec-
tively produce theorem inputs that are more coherent,
more complete, and substantially more usable under a
fixed downstream theorem-processing interface than
raw OCR text or weaker reconstruction baselines.
These findings suggest that part of the practical dif-
ficulty of mathematical formalization lies not only in
theorem translation itself, but also in the earlier step
where theorem statements are reconstructed from

A Annotation Guideline Overview
The benchmark is annotated around theorem units
rather than generic document spans. Annotators
jointly inspect the OCR JSON and rendered page
image, identify candidate theorem-like regions, re-
solve boundaries, assign unit types, attach formulas,
minimally normalize the content, and record source
provenance. Ambiguous cases include detached head-
ings, theorem–proof transitions, definition–example
interleaving, and OCR over-merging.
B Prompting and Output Schemas
All LLM-based baselines emit theorem units using
a common structured schema. Prompts are fixed on
the development split and held constant on the test
split. The LLM-only baseline reconstructs theorem-
like units directly from OCR-derived block sequences;
the hybrid baseline validates structurally proposed
spans; and the correction prompt is restricted to
minimal, structure-preserving OCR-sensitive edits.
